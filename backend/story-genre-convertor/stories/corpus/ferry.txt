Dhruba's boat was rated for eighteen and he had been running twenty-six across the Meghna for four years.

Nobody minded. The alternative was the bridge road, which was thirty-one kilometres around, and the people who used his crossing were people for whom thirty-one kilometres was a day. He took a little more than the fair price and gave a little more than the fair service, and if the boat sat low, everyone on it could see that it sat low and had made their own arithmetic before stepping on.

The storm came up on a Thursday in the second week of the rains.

He had twenty-nine aboard, which was four more than he had ever carried, because the market had been on and the light was going and everyone on that bank wanted to be on the other one. The wind arrived from the southwest with no warning that anybody who was not a boatman would have recognised. Dhruba recognised it. He was untying when he did.

A man came down the bank at a run, waving. He had a bicycle and a bag and he was shouting the fare, doubled, then tripled.

Dhruba looked at his boat. He looked at the water beyond the point, which had gone the colour of a dirty coin. He had four inches of freeboard and twenty-nine souls and a wind that was going to be on his beam for the middle third of the crossing.

He said no.

The man kept shouting. He came down onto the mud and put a hand on the gunwale, and Dhruba, who was not a cruel man, said again: no. Not for triple. Not for ten times. Take the bridge road.

He pushed off.

The crossing took fifty minutes instead of twenty. Twice the boat took water over the low side and the women bailed with their hands and a cooking pot, and a boy of about nine sat exactly where Dhruba told him to sit and did not move for the entire crossing, and they came in a kilometre downstream of the landing and everybody walked back up the bank in the dark.

Twenty-nine people. Every one of them.

The man with the bicycle took the bridge road. Eleven kilometres along it, at a place where the shoulder had gone soft, a truck coming the other way put him into the ditch. He was found the next morning.

The story that travelled was not the story of the crossing.

It was: Dhruba refused a man in a storm and the man died. It was told that way because that is a shape people can hold. The other shape — that twenty-nine lived because he stopped at twenty-nine — required you to imagine a thing that did not happen, and imagining what did not happen is work.

His brother-in-law said it first, at the funeral, quietly and not entirely unkindly. Others said it louder. Within a month it was the thing about him.

He kept running the boat. His numbers fell — not to nothing, but the market crowd started walking up to the newer boat with the engine, and the newer boat was rated for twenty-four and carried thirty, and everybody could see that too.

He never argued it. That is what people found hardest to like about him. He would listen to a man tell him to his face that he had let a fellow drown for the sake of a rule, and he would nod, and he would say the boat was full.

Four years later the newer boat went over at the point, in weather that was bad but not remarkable, with thirty-one aboard. Nineteen came out.

Nobody came to Dhruba and said they had been wrong. That is not a thing that happens. What happened was quieter: the district put a man at the landing with a clicker and a painted board, and the board said 18, and for the first year the man with the clicker was resented by everyone including Dhruba, who now could not carry twenty-six either.

He is old now. He sits at the landing most days, on the concrete, and watches the licensed boat go back and forth with its counted eighteen.

The boy who sat exactly where he was told and did not move — he is a man in his thirties with a shop in Bhairab, and once a year, in the second week of the rains, he takes the crossing he does not need to take, and pays, and sits on the concrete for an hour with an old man who does not much like being thanked.

They talk about the shop, mostly. It is understood between them that the crossing is the thanks and that saying it aloud would spend it.
