The clause was thirty-one words long and Ilse Brandt changed four of them.

She had been interpreting for the delegation for nine days. It was a supply agreement, unglamorous, the kind of document that decides how a thing gets from a mine to a factory and who carries the loss when it doesn't. On the ninth afternoon the head of the visiting side said something that was, in his language, a customary formula — the sort of thing a man says to close a difficult hour. Rendered exactly, it meant: *and if you cannot hold to this, we will make sure you are not able to hold to anything else either.*

It was not a threat. Or rather, it was a threat the way a handshake is a grip. Everyone in the room he came from would have heard it as punctuation.

The room he was in was not that room. Ilse could see the two lawyers on the near side and knew, with the peculiar certainty of a person who has watched a great many negotiations from a chair nobody looks at, that if she said it as it was said, the deal died in the next four minutes and the mine went to a company that would not bother with the water testing.

She said: *and we would consider any failure to meet these terms a serious matter between us.*

The near side nodded. The far side nodded. It was signed on Thursday.

She thought about it for perhaps a month and then stopped, the way you stop thinking about a stair you nearly fell down.

Two years later the supply failed. Not through malice — a rail line went, then a second one, and the shipments that should have gone north went nowhere for eleven weeks. And the far side invoked the clause. They invoked the version of it that existed in their language, which had never been the version the near side had agreed to, and their reading was not unreasonable given the words their own principal had spoken in that room.

The dispute went to arbitration. It was there that somebody requested the interpreter's notes.

Ilse still had them. She had always kept them; it was a discipline drilled into her at twenty-three by a woman who had interpreted at three ceasefires. Her notebook contained, in her own shorthand, the sentence as it was actually said, and beside it, in the margin, the four-word alteration and a small mark she used for *chosen*.

She could have lost the notebook. It would have taken a moment and no one would ever have asked twice.

She wrote to the arbitration secretary instead, and enclosed a copy, and explained in one page what she had done and why she had done it, and that she had done it alone and had told nobody at the time.

The finding, when it came, went against the near side on the money. The clause was construed as the far side had construed it, because the arbitrators held that a party cannot be bound by a meaning it was prevented from hearing. The near side paid, and it hurt them, and they survived it.

Ilse was removed from the accredited list. There was a hearing, and it was fair, and the panel said things she agreed with. The word they used was *substitution*, and they used it several times, and each time it landed exactly where it should.

She has not interpreted at that level since.

What changed is smaller than the loss and outlasted it. The accreditation body now requires a marked transcript for any session at that tier: where the interpreter departs from literal rendering — for register, for idiom, for a formula that would misfire — the departure is logged in the margin and the log is part of the record. It costs the interpreter about four seconds a page. Nobody reads the logs.

They exist because of one, and it is not filed under her name.

She teaches the new ones now, in a room with bad chairs, and she puts the same problem to every intake on the first morning. A man says a thing that means one thing where he stands and another where he is heard. You have four seconds.

They always ask what the right answer is.

*There isn't one,* she says. *There's only whether you write down what you did.*
