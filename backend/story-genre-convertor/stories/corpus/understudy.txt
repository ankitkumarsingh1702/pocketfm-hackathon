The call sheet said seven-fifteen. Marisol was in the wings at six.

She had been understudy to Petra Vance for two seasons, which meant she had learned every line of four productions and spoken none of them to a paying audience. On the night the thing happened, she was standing beside the stage manager's desk when the phone rang, and because the stage manager had gone to deal with a fuse, Marisol picked it up.

It was Petra. Her car had broken down on the ring road. She would be forty minutes, maybe fifty. Tell them to hold the curtain.

"I'll tell them," Marisol said.

She put the phone down. The house was filling. She could hear it through the wall, that particular sound of eight hundred people who have not yet decided to be quiet.

She did not tell them.

She stood beside the desk for four minutes and did nothing, which is not the same as doing nothing, and when the stage manager came back and said where in God's name is Vance, Marisol said nobody had called.

They put her on. There was no time to do anything else. The dresser was crying and the director was on the stairs saying the same word over and over, and Marisol went out into the light and did the thing she had rehearsed alone in her kitchen for two years.

She was good. That is the part nobody disputes. There is a review that still exists, in which a man who had seen the play four times wrote that the third act finally made sense to him, and that he had not understood until that night that the character was frightened rather than cold.

Petra arrived at the interval and watched the second half from the back of the stalls, standing, because there were no seats.

Afterwards there was the sort of party that happens when something has gone wrong and turned out well. Somebody put a glass in Marisol's hand. The director kissed her on both cheeks and called her a marvel and said the word *providence* twice.

She got through eleven days.

On the twelfth she knocked on the director's door and told him about the phone call. She told it plainly, in order, without the softening that would have made it survivable — that she had answered, that she had known, that she had counted the seconds and let them pass.

He listened with his hands flat on the desk.

Then he said: "Who else knows?"

"Nobody."

"Petra?"

"She thinks the message got lost. She thinks it was the fuse."

He was quiet for a while. He was not a cruel man and Marisol had expected, she realised, to be punished by someone who enjoyed it, and that was not what was happening.

"Here's what I want you to understand," he said. "If this is known, we lose the reviews. Not just yours — the production's. The transfer goes. Fourteen people lose a job in April. Petra doesn't get the thing she's owed for a bad two years." He turned his hands over. "And you would not work again. Not here, not anywhere that talks to here."

"I know."

"Then why did you tell me?"

"Because I couldn't decide by myself," she said. "I've been trying for eleven days and I can't."

He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something.

"Then I'll decide," he said. "It didn't happen. The message was lost in the fuse. That's the story, and I'm telling you it's the story, so it isn't your decision any more. Do you understand what I'm giving you?"

She did. He was taking it from her and putting it in a drawer of his own, and he was doing it out of something that was almost kindness, and there would be no way to ever get it back out.

She said thank you. She went to her dressing room, which was Petra's dressing room, and sat down.

She finished the run. She was excellent every night, and the notices grew, and in the second week of the transfer she handed in her notice and gave a reason that satisfied nobody.

She teaches now, in a town with one theatre. She is very good at it. There is one thing she says to every student in their first week, and she says it lightly, as a joke about the profession, and none of them hear it as anything else.

*The part you want will come to you by accident. Make sure you can look at the accident afterwards.*
