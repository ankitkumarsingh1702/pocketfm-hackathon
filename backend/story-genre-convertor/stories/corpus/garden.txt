Mrs Sequeira went to her sister's in September and asked Anita to water the pots.

It was not a large request between neighbours who had shared a wall for six years. There were nineteen pots on the back terrace and a bed along the fence, and the difficult one was a jasmine that had been struck from a cutting Mrs Sequeira's mother had carried from Goa in a lunch tin in 1961. She said this every year. Anita had heard it perhaps forty times and had never once been bored by it, which is a form of love people do not have a word for.

"Three weeks," said Mrs Sequeira. "Four at the outside."

Anita watered them. She did it properly — early, at the root, and she pinched the jasmine back in October because it was leggy and she knew what Mrs Sequeira would have said.

The three weeks became six. There was a phone call in which Mrs Sequeira's voice was strange and she said the doctors in Belgaum were being thorough, and there was another in November which was shorter.

In December a nephew came for the keys.

He was perfectly civil. He was a man of about fifty in a good shirt who had a flight on Thursday and a house to deal with, and he stood on the terrace looking at nineteen pots the way a man looks at a queue he has joined by mistake.

"She died on the ninth," he said. "I'm sorry, I assumed you'd been told."

Anita had not been told.

She asked about the garden because she could not think of what else to ask. He said the house would be sold, that it had to be, that there were four of them and none in the country. He said it kindly. He was not doing anything wrong. He said she should take anything she wanted from the terrace, and he said it in the tone of a man giving away a problem.

She took nothing that day. It felt like looting.

The sale went through in March. Anita watered the pots until the week before the handover, letting herself in with the key nobody had asked her to return, and then on the last morning she went out with a knife and took one cutting from the jasmine, four inches, at an angle, below a node.

The new people were pleasant. They had a small child and a plan.

They paved the terrace in June. It was a good job, done properly, with a drainage fall and a border of grey brick, and the nineteen pots went to the tip along with the bed along the fence and the jasmine that had come from Goa in a lunch tin. Anita heard the skip arrive. She did not go out.

The cutting took badly at first. It sulked for a year in a pot too big for it, and she nearly lost it in the cold snap, and she moved it three times before she found the corner by her kitchen door where it decided to live.

It is enormous now. It has been over the door for eleven years and in June the whole back of the house smells of it, and the woman next door — the one with the paved terrace, whose child is at university — leans over the wall every summer and says the same thing.

"That's a wonderful smell. What is it?"

And Anita says: "Jasmine. It's an old one."

She has never said the rest of it. Not because she is bitter — she stopped being bitter a long time ago, somewhere around the fourth year, when it became clear that the plant was going to live. She doesn't say it because the story requires the woman to feel something about a terrace she paved in good faith when her daughter was three, and there is nothing to be gained from that, and Mrs Sequeira, who had a hard mouth and a soft way with anything green, would have said so herself.

Every year in September, Anita takes a cutting. Four inches, at an angle, below a node.

She gives them away. She has given away nine or ten. They are in gardens in three countries now, in the pots of people who know only that a woman on their street grows a good jasmine and is generous with it.

She never explains where it came from. It seems to her that a thing carried in a lunch tin in 1961 by a woman who is now dead does not need her explaining it, only her doing the next bit.
