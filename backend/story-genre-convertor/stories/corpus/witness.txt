There were two men on the loading dock that night and Rukmini saw both of them.

She was nineteen and worked the late shift at the packing plant, and what she saw took four seconds: the shorter man swinging something short and heavy, and the other man going down against the bollard, and the shorter man walking away at a pace that was not running.

She gave a statement. She named him. His name was Sattar and he supplied the plant with pallets and everyone knew him.

The first visit was three days later and it was her mother who answered the door. It was not a threat. A man stood on the step with a bag of oranges and said he was a friend of Sattar's family and that these things were often confused at night, and that Rukmini's brother worked at the depot on the Bhiwandi road, didn't he, which was a long way for a boy to come home in the dark.

He left the oranges. Her mother put them in a bowl and neither of them ate any.

Rukmini went back to the police in the second week and said she had been mistaken. She said the light was bad. She said she had seen a man of that build and had assumed. The inspector, who was not stupid, asked her three times whether anybody had spoken to her, and three times she said no.

They convicted Sattar anyway. There was a partial print on the bollard and a man in a car across the road who had seen less than Rukmini but had nobody at his door with oranges. Nine years.

That should have been the end of it, and it was not, because Rukmini had learned something about herself that she could not put down: that when it was put to her plainly, she had chosen her brother's walk home over a dead man's name, and that she would probably choose it again, and that being a person who would probably choose it again was going to be the weather of her whole life.

She married. She worked. She did not talk about it.

Eleven years later a lawyer found her.

Sattar was out and had been out for two years, and was trying to have the conviction quashed, and the argument was that the print was contaminated and the man in the car was unreliable. And the lawyer had read the file and found a nineteen-year-old who had named his client on a Tuesday and unnamed him on a Friday, and had come to ask, in the careful way of someone who has already guessed the answer, why.

Rukmini understood the shape of it immediately. If she said nothing, Sattar's appeal turned on a bad print, and bad prints win. If she said what had happened, the withdrawal became evidence of intimidation, and intimidation is the one thing that closes an appeal like a door.

But saying it meant saying the other part. That she had lied to an inspector who asked her three times. That she had signed it.

She asked for a day. She was given a week.

She went in on the Monday and told it in order, all of it, including the oranges and including the three denials, to a woman from the prosecutor's office who wrote steadily and did not look up.

The appeal failed. Sattar remains convicted.

They charged Rukmini. Perjury has a long tail and hers had been a signed statement, and the fact that the second statement was the false one did not make the offence smaller, only sadder.

Her lawyer wanted to run duress and she would not let him. Duress would have meant putting the man with the oranges in a courtroom, and that man had a family and had, so far as she could establish in eleven years of not looking, never done anything worse than stand on a step. It also would have meant asking a court to agree that she'd had no choice, and she had come too far to say that about herself.

She pleaded. She got fourteen months and served seven.

Her brother, who is forty now and still works the Bhiwandi road, has never entirely forgiven her, on the grounds that she went to prison to correct a thing she had done to protect him and never asked whether he wanted protecting.

She tells him he is right about that. She tells him it is the one part she would do differently.

She does not say which part she means, and he has stopped asking.
