Priya Rao found the ampoules in a wash bag in the third week of the camp.

She was not snooping. She had gone into the shared bathroom for a plaster and the bag was open on the shelf, and she saw what was in it, and she knew what it was because every athlete at that level knows what it is. The bag belonged to Meera Kulkarni, who had been half a second behind her for six years and eleven hundredths behind her in Bhubaneswar.

She stood there for a while.

That evening Meera came and found her by the water tank and did not bother with denial. She was very calm about it. She said her father had been ill for two years and that the sponsorship was the only thing paying for it, and that she was twenty-nine and had one more season in her legs and that Priya, who was twenty-four and faster, could afford a conscience.

Then she made the offer. There was a selection meeting in November for the relay squad, and Meera's coach sat on it, and Meera's coach thought Priya was arrogant, and Meera could change that in an afternoon.

Priya said she would think about it.

She thought about it for eleven days and then said nothing to anybody, which is the same as accepting, and both of them understood that.

She made the relay squad in November. Her coach told her the room had turned around and he did not know why.

She won the 800 in Guwahati in June, and she won it clean — that is the part that survived, in the sense that nothing about her own blood was ever in question, before or after. She had never taken anything. She has never taken anything.

Meera was caught in August. Not by a test at a meet; by a whereabouts failure that turned into an audit that turned into eighteen months of records. It was thorough and it was slow and it was, by the time it finished, very public.

Somewhere in it was Meera's phone.

There was a message from the third week of the camp, sent at 11:40 at night, that said: *she saw. it's handled.* And there was a reply from the coach that said nothing incriminating at all, and a second message four days later that said: *rao is fine. she's not going to do anything.*

The panel called Priya in October.

She could have said she had not understood what she saw. It would have been thin but not impossible; she was twenty-four, the bag was not hers, and no test had ever touched her. Her federation lawyer laid this out for her in a corridor and was not being corrupt in doing it — he was describing a real option that a great many people would have taken.

She told them what happened. All of it: the bathroom, the water tank, the offer, the eleven days, the November meeting she had been given.

The rule she had broken has a number and the number is dull. Failure to report a known violation. It carries less than doping does, and more than nothing, and it does not care whether you were paid in money or in a seat on a committee.

Two years. The Guwahati result stood, because she had not doped, but the relay medal went, because the squad she made had been made for her.

She was twenty-seven when she came back and the half-second she had always had was gone. She ran two more seasons and finished fourth, seventh, fourth.

She coaches now, at a district centre with an eight-lane track and a broken timing gate.

There is a talk she gives to every intake, and she gives it on the first day rather than saving it, and it is not the talk they expect from someone with her history. She does not talk about doping. They have all heard about doping.

She tells them that someone will one day offer them something for their silence, and that it will not feel like a bribe, because it will be offered by a person they like who is having a worse year than they are.

And she tells them the thing she wishes someone had told her at twenty-four, which is that the eleven days are the offence. Not the answer at the end of them.
