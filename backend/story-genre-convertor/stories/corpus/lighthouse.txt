The automation notice came in April and gave them until the end of the year.

Halloran had kept the light at Ruadh Head for twenty-two years and his father had kept it for nineteen before him, and the letter explained, in four paragraphs of considerable politeness, that a machine would do it from January and that his service was valued.

He did not argue. There was nothing to argue with; the machine would in fact do it, and would do it in weather that had twice nearly killed him.

What he did instead was start the log.

There had always been a log — every keeper kept one, wind and visibility and the hours the light burned, in a series of identical ledgers going back to 1874. Halloran had filled eleven of them himself. What he began in April was different: he started writing down the things the ledger had never had a column for.

That the swell out of the northwest made a particular sound against the base at half tide and a different one at full, and that you could hear the difference from the kitchen with the door shut, and that this was how you knew the wind had backed before any instrument said so.

That the third step of the tower stair had worn in a way that told you, in the dark, that you were three from the bottom.

That a boat in trouble does not usually signal. That it goes quiet and then does something stupid, and that the stupid thing is the signal.

That there was a particular quality of fog — thin, moving, at about forty feet — that would make the light look like it was failing from the water even though it was not, and that in that fog you rang the bell whether the regulations required it or not, because a skipper who thinks the light is failing will do the stupid thing.

He wrote it in the evenings from April to December, in a hardbacked notebook that was not one of the official ledgers. Two hundred and thirty pages. He was not a writer and it reads like a man explaining a machine to someone standing next to him.

In December he handed over the station. The engineer who came was competent and young and had a checklist, and Halloran walked him through the equipment for two days and answered everything he was asked.

He did not give him the notebook. The engineer had not asked for one, and the checklist had no line for it, and Halloran had discovered in himself an unwillingness to hand over two hundred and thirty pages to a man who would have had to be polite about them.

He took it with him to the house in Portnahaven.

He was there eleven years. He died in February and his daughter came up from Glasgow and cleared the house, and the notebook was in a box with the eleven official ledgers, which she offered to the Northern Lighthouse Board because they were clearly historical.

The Board took the ledgers. The archivist, going through the box, opened the notebook, read four pages standing up, and then sat down and read the rest.

It is not in a museum. That is the thing worth knowing about it.

It was typed up, and the section on fog and the section on the sound of the swell were put into the training material for the crews who service the automated stations — men who arrive by boat, work for six hours, and leave, and who need to know when the water at the base is telling them something. Two paragraphs of it went into a safety notice in 2011 after an incident at a different station in exactly the fog Halloran had described.

The notice does not carry his name. It carries a reference number.

His daughter asked about that once, at the time, and the archivist explained the format apologetically, and she said it was fine, and afterwards she thought about it and decided it was in fact fine, and this is why.

Her father had spent twenty-two years keeping a light that told ships where the rocks were. It had never told them who was keeping it. He would, she thought, have found the reference number entirely correct — a thing that works, in the dark, for people who do not need to know your name.
