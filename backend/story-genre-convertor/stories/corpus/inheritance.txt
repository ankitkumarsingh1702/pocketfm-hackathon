The lawyer read the will in a room that smelled of furniture polish and old rain. There was not much to divide. The house on Cross Lane, a savings account with less in it than anyone expected, and a workshop full of tools nobody knew how to price.

It went to Nalini. All of it.

Across the table, Devika did not move. She was the older one, the one who had stayed, the one who had learned to lift a grown man from a bed without waking him. For eleven years she had measured out pills into a plastic tray with seven compartments and washed sheets at two in the morning and told the same four stories to a man who forgot them each time. Nalini had come at festivals. Nalini had sent money, which was not nothing, and had gone home again, which was not nothing either.

"There's no contest here," the lawyer said. "It's clear."

"It's clear," Devika agreed.

She cleared out the workshop the following week because nobody else was going to. It was in the third drawer of the bench, under a tin of screws, folded into quarters: a single sheet in her father's hand, dated fourteen months ago, when he could still hold a pen if you braced his wrist. It left the house to her. It said, in the flat way he said everything, that Devika had given up years she would not get back and that this was the only payment available. It was signed. Two neighbours had witnessed it. One of them had died since, but one had not.

Devika sat on the workshop floor for a long time.

Then she put the page in her coat pocket and did not mention it.

She told herself she was waiting for the right moment, and she went on telling herself that for two months while Nalini put the house on the market, and while the board went up, and while strangers walked through the kitchen saying the units would have to go. Every day the page was in the pocket of a coat she had stopped wearing. Every day she did not take it out.

What ended it was ordinary. Nalini came to collect the last of the linen and found the coat on the back of the door and moved it, and the paper fell out, and she picked it up because paper on a floor gets picked up.

She read it twice.

"How long?"

"Since the week after."

"Two months." Nalini's voice did not rise, which was worse. "You let me list it. You watched me stand in the garden with an estate agent."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you burn it? If you were going to do this, why not do it properly?"

Devika had asked herself the same thing and had an answer she did not like. "Because I wanted you to find it," she said. "I wanted it to happen without me having to be the one who did it."

Nalini sat down on the stairs with the page in her hand. Outside, someone's car alarm went off and stopped.

"He knew," Nalini said eventually. "He knew what he was doing to me. Making me the one who gets it and then leaving this where you'd find it."

"He was ill. He wasn't making a plan."

"He was always making a plan." She looked at the signature for a while. "Is it valid? Would it stand?"

"The lawyer says probably. There's a living witness."

"Then take it."

Devika did not answer.

"Take it," Nalini said again. "I'm telling you to take it. That's what you want, isn't it? For me to hand it over so you don't have to ask."

And that, precisely, was the thing. Devika looked at her sister sitting on the stairs of a house neither of them had chosen, holding a piece of paper that would settle everything and settle nothing, and understood that she could have the house or she could have a sister who did not think of her as a person who hid things in coats.

She took the page. She tore it once, and then again, and put the pieces in her pocket where it had been.

"Don't," said Nalini. "Don't do that so you can be the good one."

"I'm not the good one." Devika sat down on the step below her. "I hid it for two months. That's what I am. I'd just rather be that with you than without you."

The house sold in March. They split it, which no document required and no lawyer arranged. Devika kept the tools. She could not price them either, but she knew what each one was for, which turned out to be a different kind of knowing.
