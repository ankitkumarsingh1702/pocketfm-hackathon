They swapped seats because of a window.

The 6:40 out of Sealdah was full and Tomas had a seat facing backwards, which made him ill, and the man across the aisle had a forward seat and did not care either way. It took nine seconds to arrange. Neither of them looked at the other's ticket, because the seats were the same class and the swap was the whole transaction.

The train split at Ranaghat. It always had. The front six coaches went one way and the rear five went another, and the announcement was made twice in two languages, and both men were asleep.

Tomas woke in a town called Nabadwip with ninety minutes to spare before an interview he had prepared for over five weeks. He was not supposed to be in Nabadwip. He was supposed to be in Krishnanagar, forty minutes away, and there was no train back that would do it.

Outside the station a man with a jeep was waiting for somebody who had not come. He took Tomas for eleven hundred rupees, which was most of what he had, and drove like a man settling a grudge, and Tomas walked into the office at 9:51 for a 9:45 appointment with his shirt stuck to his back.

The panel liked him. There is no accounting for it. The lateness became the first thing he explained and therefore the first thing that made him a person rather than a CV, and the man in the middle chair had grown up two stations from Nabadwip and asked him whether the tea stall by the ticket window still had the blue awning. It did. Tomas had bought tea under it forty minutes earlier.

He got the job. It was the job that led to the next one and the one after that, and by the time he was forty he was a man other men rearranged their diaries for.

The other man's name was Anwar, and Anwar had been going to Krishnanagar too.

His father-in-law had died on the Tuesday. The burial was at eleven and the family had held it as late as the rites allowed, and Anwar arrived at ten past two to a house with the chairs already stacked. His wife did not speak to him that evening. She spoke to him again eventually, and they had two more children and a long marriage, but there was a thing that had gone out of it, and both of them could have named the date.

Anwar never knew about the swap. He assumed he had slept through the announcement, which he had.

They met again nineteen years later at a wedding in Barrackpore, in the way people do, and it took most of an evening for the coincidence to surface — the train, the split, the date, which Anwar knew exactly and Tomas knew exactly, for opposite reasons.

Tomas understood first. He watched it arrive in the other man's face a few seconds later.

"You were in the front," Anwar said. "You were in my seat."

"Yes."

They stood with their plates. The band was doing something loud and no one was listening to them.

"I've had a good life," Tomas said. "I want to be honest that I've had a good life."

"I know. I asked about you before I came over."

"Whatever it is," Tomas said. "Whatever you want. Say it now, because I'll say yes now and I don't know what I'll be in a month."

Anwar thought about it for a long time, and Tomas let him, and the band finished and started something else.

"Nothing," Anwar said at last.

"That isn't—"

"I'm not being noble. Listen." He put his plate down. "If you give me something, then it was a transaction and I was owed. I don't want to have been owed. I want it to have been nine seconds and a window, because that's what it was, and everything after it is mine." He looked at Tomas properly. "You take money off a man and it means he did it to you. Nobody did it to me."

"Then what do you want?"

"Come to my house," Anwar said. "Meet my wife. She's spent nineteen years thinking her husband slept through the one announcement that mattered, and I'd like her to hear it wasn't that."

Tomas went the following Sunday. He stayed four hours. He told it plainly to a woman who listened with her hands folded and asked two questions, both about the jeep.

Afterwards she said: "So he was awake."

"No," said Tomas. "We were both asleep. I just woke up somewhere better."
