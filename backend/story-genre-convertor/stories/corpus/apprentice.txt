Bettany's hands went in the winter of his sixty-eighth year. Not all at once — there was a tremor first, and for a while he worked around it, bracing the heel of his palm against the board and taking the long strokes on the exhale, and then in February he could not lay a straight line at all.

Corin had been with him eleven years. He had come as a boy who could copy anything and no one had ever taught him to want anything else.

"There's a commission," Bettany said. "The Ashfield chapel. Twelve panels."

"You can't do twelve panels."

"No."

They looked at each other across the workshop. Outside, the yard was full of the flat grey light that Bettany had spent forty years learning to keep out of a room.

"You'd sign them," Corin said. "In your hand."

"They're commissioned from me. They were commissioned from me two years ago, when I could. The chapel has paid half." He set his hands flat on the board to still them. "You've painted my hand since you were nineteen. There isn't a mark I make that you can't make."

"That isn't the question."

"No," Bettany agreed. "The question is whether you'll do it."

He did it.

It took fourteen months. He painted them in Bettany's manner, which had become as natural to him as his own — more natural, because his own had never been allowed to develop into anything that needed suppressing. He did not imitate. That was the strange discovery of that year: at some point the imitation had stopped being imitation and become a language he was fluent in, and a fluent speaker says new things.

The eighth panel is the one they write about. It is a Deposition, and it does something with the angle of the ladder that nobody had done, and it is the reason the Ashfield cycle is in the books at all. Bettany looked at it for a long time when Corin brought it through, and said only, "Not there. Two inches left," and Corin said no, and Bettany looked again and said, "No. You're right."

They were installed in the spring. Bettany signed each one in the corner, slowly, with Corin's hand steadying his wrist, which meant the signatures were true and false in a proportion nobody could ever calculate.

He died in the autumn.

What Corin had not expected was the ledger. Bettany kept one — every workshop did — and in it, in his own hand, going back eleven years, was a record of who had laid what. It was ordinary bookkeeping. It was also, from February onward, a complete account of who had painted the Ashfield panels, entry by entry, panel by panel, in the master's own writing.

Bettany had not destroyed it. He had left it in the drawer where it always was, on top, where anyone clearing the workshop would find it in the first hour.

Corin understood what he was being offered. It was the whole of a life, handed over quietly, with no instruction attached, because an instruction would have made it a demand.

He sat with the ledger for two days.

Then he took the pages from February onward and burned them in the yard, one at a time, in the flat grey light.

He could not have explained it to a court and did not much try to explain it to himself. Something in him understood that the panels were the work of a man who had spent forty years learning what to keep out of a room, and of a boy who had learned it from him, and that the proportion between those two was not a thing a ledger could hold. To claim it would be to state a proportion. To state a proportion would be to lie about it.

He ran the workshop for thirty years. He trained four apprentices, and he kept a ledger, and he wrote in it every evening including the evenings when there was nothing to write.

In the last year of his life a young historian came about the Ashfield cycle, because the eighth panel had begun to trouble people — the hand seemed later, she said, the ladder seemed to belong to a different decade.

Corin made her tea and answered every question she asked, precisely and at length, about pigment and ground and the difficulty of the north wall.

She never asked the one that mattered, and he had decided long before that he would answer it if she did.
