THE PROVENANCE

Priya had wanted the job at the Kalighat Institute for six years before she got
it, and for the first eleven months she could not quite believe it was hers. The
archive occupied three floors of a building that had once been a customs house,
and the collection — nineteen thousand objects, most of them small, most of them
undocumented — was the largest of its kind in the eastern half of the country.
Devraj Iyer had built it. He had been its director for twenty-two years, and it
was Devraj who had pulled her application out of a stack of sixty and told the
board she was the one.

He gave her the bronzes. That was the first thing he did after she started, and
she understood what it meant: the bronzes were the collection's spine, the
objects that brought scholars from Leiden and Chicago, and nobody under forty had
been allowed near them in a decade. "You'll catalogue them properly," he said.
"Nobody has, not since I did it myself, and I did it badly because I was in a
hurry and I was thirty-one." He laughed at that. He laughed easily. "Take two
years. Take three. It's the only thing here that will outlive us."

She took nine months to find the problem.

It was a small one, at first, and she assumed it was hers. Object 4471, a seated
figure, twelve centimetres, acquired 1998. The accession card said it came from
the estate of a collector in Chennai, a man named Rajan Pillai, who had died in
1996 and whose heirs had sold the contents of his house through a dealer. The
card gave the dealer's name and the date of the sale. Priya, who was thorough in
a way she had been told more than once was excessive, wrote to the dealer's
successor firm to ask for the original sale documents, because she wanted the lot
numbers for the catalogue entry.

The firm wrote back to say there had been no such sale. They had handled the
Pillai estate, yes. But the estate had contained no bronzes at all.

She checked the card again. She checked her transcription. She checked whether
there was a second Pillai. Then she pulled fourteen other cards at random from
the bronzes, all of them in Devraj's handwriting, all of them from the years
between 1994 and 2003, and she wrote to nine more dealers and two auction houses
and a widow in Pune.

Four of the eleven replied that the transaction described had not happened.

Priya did not tell anyone for six weeks. She told herself this was because she
needed to be certain, and that was partly true, but it was not the whole of it.
Every morning she came in and unlocked the bronze room and worked through the
cards one by one, and every evening she went home carrying a thing she did not
want to be carrying. There were, in the end, thirty-one objects whose stated
provenance could not have happened. All thirty-one had entered the collection
between 1994 and 2003. All thirty-one had cards in the same hand.

What the false provenances had in common was more damning than the fact of them.
Each one placed the object inside India before 1972. Each one, if true, made the
object legal to hold. If false, every one of the thirty-one had been dug up and
moved out of the ground and across a border in a decade when doing so was a
crime, and the Institute was holding them, and the Institute's director had
written the paperwork that made them look clean.

She went to Meenakshi Rao, who had been the conservator for thirty years and who
was the only person in the building Priya trusted with something she could not
take back. Meenakshi listened without interrupting, which took a while, and then
sat for a long moment with her hands flat on the table.

"Don't take this to the board," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because two of them were on the acquisitions committee in 1997, and neither of
them is going to thank you." She looked up. "I'm not telling you to drop it. I'm
telling you that if you walk in there with thirty-one cards, you will be the one
who leaves the building. Go to him first. He may have an answer. People
sometimes do."

So Priya went to him.

She had rehearsed it and none of the rehearsals survived contact. She sat down in
his office with the folder and she said, "I wrote to the dealers," and Devraj
looked at the folder and then at her, and something in his face went quiet and
stayed quiet, and she understood before he said a word that there was no answer
coming.

"How many?" he said.

"Thirty-one."

"There are thirty-four." He said it without any particular weight. "You'll find
the other three eventually. Two in the ivories, one in the coins."

She had prepared for denial. She had prepared for anger. She had not prepared for
arithmetic.

"They'd have gone to Zurich," he said. "All of them. That's not a defence, it's
just what would have happened. In 1996 there was a man in Trichy with a truck and
a list, and the choice in front of me was that they leave the country and are
never seen again by anyone in this country, or they come here and get a card that
lets them stay. I wrote the cards. I knew what I was writing." He turned his hand
over. "I would like to tell you I agonised. I didn't, particularly. I did it
thirty-four times."

"You have to report it."

"To whom? The board that appointed me?" He almost smiled. "Here is what will
happen if this leaves the room. Not to me — I'm sixty-eight, they'll take my name
off the wall and that will be the end of it. The thirty-four go into a repository
in Delhi for the eleven years it takes to litigate them, and about a third of
them will not survive the storage, because I have seen that repository. The
donors leave. The Leiden project ends. The building goes." He paused. "Or you
finish the bronze catalogue. You write the entries as I wrote the cards. In four
years I retire, and I recommend you, and the board listens to me. And the
objects stay where someone is looking after them."

"You're offering me your job."

"I'm offering you the collection," he said. "The job is what it costs."

Priya sat with that for what felt like a long time and was probably eight
seconds. Then she said no.

She wrote it up over the following month — thirty-four objects, the
correspondence, the four dealer letters, the two she had found in the ivories and
the one in the coins, exactly where he had said they would be. She sent it to the
board and copied the Archaeological Survey, because Meenakshi had been right
about the board.

Devraj resigned in March without contesting any of it. He did not speak to her
again. In September the board offered her the directorship, and she took it,
because by then the repository in Delhi had the thirty-four, and she had seen the
repository, and he had been right about that too.
