THE HEAD OF THE CANAL

The orchard had two hundred and forty trees and it needed water eleven times a
year, and for three generations it had got that water from the Nandur channel,
which came off the main canal at a sluice gate half a kilometre above Sulekha's
land. The sluice gate sat on Bhaskar Deshmukh's property. It had sat there since
before either of them was born, and for most of that time it had not mattered
whose property it sat on, because the water came and everyone took their share
and nobody counted.

Then there were two bad monsoons in a row, and everybody counted.

Bhaskar closed the gate in the second week of April. He did not close it all the
way, which was the clever part. He left it open a hand's width, enough that the
channel below still ran, enough that anyone standing at Sulekha's field could see
water in it and would have a hard time explaining to a magistrate that they were
being denied. It was simply not enough water for two hundred and forty trees in
April.

Sulekha went up to see him on the fourth day.

"It's the same gate it's always been," Bhaskar said. "I haven't touched it."

"You've touched it."

"Show me a paper that says how far open it has to be." He was not being cruel
about it, which she found worse than if he had been. "There isn't one. There has
never been one. For sixty years my father and your father did it on a
handshake, and I'm not saying that was wrong, I'm saying my grandsons are going
to farm this land after me and a handshake isn't going to be there."

What he wanted was not water. He had water. What he wanted was the eleven acres
of flat land at the bottom of Sulekha's holding, the piece along the road, which
was the only part of her land you could get a truck onto and the only part worth
anything to a man who wanted to stop farming and start renting to a warehouse
company. He had asked her for it twice in four years. She had said no twice.

"Sell me the road piece," he said, "and I'll sign you a right to the channel.
Properly. Registered. In writing, forever, so nobody's grandsons have to
negotiate it again."

She said she would think about it.

She thought about it for nine days, during which the trees began to show it. Her
son Ashwin wanted to go to the water regulator, and she let him, and it went the
way she had expected: the regulator's office said the Nandur channel was a
private arrangement below the main canal and outside their authority, and that
she was welcome to file a civil suit, and that the current backlog in the
district civil court was somewhere between four and six years. The trees had six
weeks.

On the tenth day she went back up the hill and told Bhaskar she would sell.

They did it properly, or it looked properly. His lawyer drew two documents: a
sale deed for the eleven acres and a deed of easement giving her holding a
perpetual right to water from the Nandur channel. They signed both in the
sub-registrar's office in town on a Thursday, and Bhaskar was courteous the whole
morning, and he shook her hand on the steps afterwards and said it was better
this way, that it should have been written down years ago.

She got eleven days of water. On the twelfth day the channel was dry.

She walked the half kilometre up and found the sluice gate shut and a second
thing that had not been there before: a new cut, upstream of the gate, a rough
diversion running east across Bhaskar's own land toward his cane. The easement
gave her a perpetual right to water *from the Nandur channel*. It did not say a
word about how much water had to enter the Nandur channel from the main canal,
because the main canal offtake was a separate structure with a separate history,
and Bhaskar's lawyer had known that, and Sulekha had not.

"The channel's yours," Bhaskar said, when she found him. "I've not taken one drop
out of the channel. Read it."

She read it. He had not.

Ashwin wanted to go back to the regulator, and to the police, and to a lawyer in
Pune, and to the newspaper. Sulekha told him to sit down. Then she went into the
steel almirah in the back room and took out the folder her father had kept, which
she had inherited along with the trees and had never had any reason to open.

Her father had been on the canal committee in 1971. When the main canal was
widened, every landholder along it had signed a schedule of works, and the
schedule specified, offtake by offtake, the structures the department was
permitted to build and the ones it was not. Sulekha's father had signed it.
Bhaskar's father had signed it. And the schedule contained a clause, in the flat
unlovely language of a government form, prohibiting any new offtake or diversion
above an existing sanctioned offtake without the written consent of every holding
below it.

The cut Bhaskar had dug was above the sanctioned Nandur offtake. It was, on
paper, thirty years newer than the document that forbade it. And the document had
been filed with the department, which meant it was not her word against his — it
was a departmental record, sitting in an office in the district headquarters,
where it had been sitting since before either of them could read.

She did not go to Bhaskar. She went to the regulator, with a certified copy, and
the regulator's office — which had told her six weeks earlier that the channel
was none of its business — discovered that an unsanctioned diversion off the main
canal was very much its business. They served notice in nine days. Bhaskar had
the cut filled at his own cost within the month, and the notice went on the
record of his holding, where it stayed, and where the warehouse company's lawyers
found it when they came to look at the eleven acres along the road.

The warehouse company withdrew. Bhaskar came down the hill in August and offered
to sell the eleven acres back to her at what he had paid.

Sulekha said no. She had the water; the easement he had drafted so carefully was
now worth exactly what he had promised it was worth, because the diversion was
gone and the channel ran full. And she found that she did not want the road piece
back, or him unmade, or the thing settled with a handshake.

"I've got a paper," she said. "You were right about that much."
