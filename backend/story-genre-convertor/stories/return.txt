SIXTH STREET

Ismail had been away nineteen years and had thought about the house on most of
the days of them. It was the one fixed thing. Whatever else he was — a man
sharing a flat with two strangers in Sharjah at twenty-four, a foreman at
thirty-one, a man with a bad back and a small amount of money at forty-three —
he was also a man who owned a house on Sixth Street, three rooms and a courtyard
and a lime tree, and everything he did was arranged around going back to it.

He had not been able to go back before. There were reasons every year, and the
reasons were real, and they were also, he understood by about year twelve, a
thing he was doing on purpose, because as long as he did not go the house stayed
exactly as he had left it.

His father had died in the eleventh year. Ismail had not come for the funeral;
the visa took six weeks and the funeral took two days. After that his uncle
Yusuf, who lived four streets over, had looked after the place — swept it,
opened the shutters, kept the tax paid out of the money Ismail sent every
Ramadan. Yusuf wrote twice a year, short letters in a careful hand, and the
letters always said the same three things: the family was well, the house was
well, come when you can.

He came in October, off the night bus with one suitcase, and walked from the
stand because the walk was part of the thing he had been imagining.

There were children in the courtyard.

He stood at the gate for some time. The lime tree was gone and there was a
concrete water tank where it had been. Someone had painted the front rooms a pale
green and put a steel door where the wooden one had been. A woman came out, saw
him, and did not seem alarmed, which he found he minded.

"I'm looking for Yusuf Sheikh," he said.

"He doesn't live here."

"No. This is my house."

The woman's face changed, but not in the way he expected. She did not look
guilty. She looked tired, in the specific way of someone who has had this
conversation before and did not enjoy it the first time.

"You should talk to my husband," she said.

Her husband was called Faraz and he had bought the house four years earlier, and
he had the deed, and he showed it to Ismail at the table in what had been his
mother's room, and it was in order. Registered. Stamped. Sold by Yusuf Sheikh,
acting under a power of attorney, for a price that was low but not absurdly low
for that street in that year.

"He showed me the paper," Faraz said. "It was witnessed. I checked at the
registrar. I'm not — " He stopped and started again. "I'm sorry. I don't know
what to tell you. I have four years in this house and two children born in it."

Ismail went to Yusuf's.

His uncle was seventy-one and had had a stroke sixteen months before and could
use one hand. He was sitting in the front room with a shawl over his legs and he
looked up when Ismail came in and his face did something complicated, and Ismail
understood he had been waiting for this for four years and had probably rehearsed
it, and that the stroke had taken the rehearsal away.

"I sold it," Yusuf said.

"You had no right."

"I had the paper. Your father gave me the paper." The good hand moved toward a
cupboard, and stopped. "In the cupboard. Take it."

Ismail took it. It was a general power of attorney over the property on Sixth
Street, executed by his father, registered, dated. He read the date twice. It was
from the year before his father died — three years before Ismail's father had
last written to him, and two years before the letter in which his father had
said, in the same words he always used, *the house is here, it is not going
anywhere.*

"He wanted it sold then," Yusuf said. "Nineteen ninety-eight. He had debts, and
he was ill, and he said to me, sell it and send the boy the money and let him
stop sending money home. And I said no. I said, don't do that to him. And he
said, then don't tell him. And I didn't. And then he died and I kept it for
eleven more years."

"You could have written."

"I wrote twice a year for nineteen years."

"You could have written *that*."

"Yes," Yusuf said.

He had sold it in the end because his own son's wife had needed a surgery in
Hyderabad and there was no other money, and because he had been the caretaker of
an empty building for eleven years for a man who never came, and at some point,
he said, without excusing it, he had stopped believing Ismail was ever going to
come.

Ismail spent nine days at a lodge near the bus stand. He went to two lawyers. The
first told him the sale was probably void because a general power of attorney does
not survive the death of the person who gave it, and that he could file, and that
Faraz would fight it because Faraz had nowhere else to go, and that it would take
years and he would very likely win. The second told him the same thing and then
asked what he wanted, and when Ismail could not answer, the lawyer said: "Then
don't file yet. Filing is easy. Stopping isn't."

On the eighth day he walked back to Sixth Street and stood at the gate again and
watched the older of the two children — a girl, six or seven — dragging a chair
across the courtyard to reach something on a shelf, in the exact spot where the
lime tree had been, and he found that what he was feeling was not what he had
walked there expecting to feel.

The house he owned had stopped existing sometime around the third or fourth year
he was away. What was on Sixth Street was a different house that happened to
stand in the same place, and he had been sending money to a memory, and his
father had known that in 1998 and had tried to tell him by not telling him.

He did not file. He went to see Yusuf once more, and they did not talk about the
house, and then he took the night bus back and flew out on the Thursday. He kept
the deed his father had signed. He has it still. He has never entirely decided
whether that is because he might use it, or because it is the only thing left
that has the house in it.
